# Phase 2 — Original Authorship & History

This file records, for each major proof or tradition, who is credited, the
approximate date, and the primary source — and carefully **separates documented
history from legend**. A persistent feature of this theorem's history is that its
most famous attribution (to Pythagoras) is **traditional, not documented**.

---

## The "Pythagoras" attribution is legend, not record

- **No surviving text by Pythagoras (c. 570–495 BCE) exists**, and none from his
  lifetime credits him with the theorem or any proof. The Pythagoreans kept
  doctrines oral and secret.
- The attribution comes from **much later** sources: Proclus (5th c. CE), in his
  commentary on Euclid, and Plutarch, Diogenes Laertius, etc. — writing
  ~700–1000 years after Pythagoras. Proclus himself is cautious and reports the
  tradition (including the legend of Pythagoras sacrificing oxen) at second hand.
- Scholarly consensus (e.g., Walter Burkert): the geometric theorem and its
  proof cannot be securely attributed to the historical Pythagoras. The name is a
  label for a school and a tradition.

**Verdict:** "Pythagorean theorem" is a convention. Treat the personal
attribution as **undocumented legend**.

---

## Babylonian and Egyptian prehistory (knowledge of the relation, not a proof)

- **Plimpton 322** (Old Babylonian, c. 1800 BCE): a clay tablet listing what are
  effectively **Pythagorean triples** (or the ratios behind them). It shows the
  *numerical relation* was known and systematically tabulated over a millennium
  before Pythagoras — but it is a table, **not a proof**.
- **YBC 7289** (Old Babylonian): a tablet giving the diagonal of a unit square as
  a sexagesimal approximation of `sqrt(2)` to ~6 decimal digits — again, the
  relation in use, no proof.
- Egyptian "rope-stretchers" (harpedonaptai) reportedly used 3–4–5 ropes for
  right angles; evidence is indirect and there is no Egyptian proof on record.

**Verdict:** the *relation* is far older than Greek geometry; a *deductive proof*
is a Greek (and independently Chinese) contribution.

---

## Chinese tradition — the *gougu* rule

- **Zhoubi Suanjing (周髀算經)**, compiled in the **Han period** (roughly 200 BCE–
  100 CE, with older material). It states the **gougu** rule (gou = shorter leg,
  gu = longer leg, xian = hypotenuse) and is accompanied by the **hsuan-thu /
  xian-tu (弦圖)** diagram.
- **Zhao Shuang (趙爽), 3rd c. CE**, wrote the commentary giving the dissection
  argument (Family A1) — a genuine proof, developed **independently of the Greek
  tradition** and roughly contemporaneous with late antiquity.
- **Liu Hui (劉徽), 263 CE**, commentary on the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical
  Art*, gives related dissection ("out–in complementarity") arguments.

**Verdict:** the Chinese proof tradition is independent and the dissection proof
there is documented to a named commentator (Zhao Shuang), unlike the Greek
personal attribution.

---

## Greek tradition — Euclid

- **Euclid, *Elements*, Book I, Proposition 47** (c. 300 BCE), with the converse
  at I.48. The "windmill/bride's-chair" area proof (Family B1) is **the earliest
  fully rigorous, documented proof in the Western canon**, and the *Elements* is
  its primary source. Euclid VI.31 generalizes it to similar figures on the sides.
- Whether I.47 is Euclid's own proof or an inherited one is unknown; what is
  documented is that it appears in the *Elements*.

---

## India — Bhaskara and the *sulba* tradition

- The **Sulba Sutras** (Baudhayana, c. 800–600 BCE) state the theorem for the
  diagonal of a rectangle in the context of altar construction — a statement,
  with at most a verification, not a deductive proof.
- **Bhaskara II (Bhaskaracharya), 12th c. CE**, gives the dissection "Behold!"
  proof (Family A2) in the *Lilavati / Bijaganita* tradition.

---

## Modern named proofs

- **James A. Garfield, 1876** — the trapezoid proof (Family D1), published in the
  *New England Journal of Education*. Garfield was a **sitting member of the U.S.
  House of Representatives** at the time (elected President in 1880). The proof is
  genuinely his contribution and well documented. *Not* a misattribution.
- **"Einstein's proof"** — the similarity/area-scaling proof (Family C2) is
  popularly attributed to Einstein as a teenager (recounted in a letter and by
  biographers). The *argument* is older (it is essentially Euclid VI / the
  similarity proof); the attribution to a young Einstein is **anecdotal**, and the
  proof is not original to him. Treat as **traditional/anecdotal attribution**.
- **Leonardo da Vinci** is credited with a symmetry-based dissection proof
  (documented in the Renaissance manuscripts tradition); the attribution is
  commonly repeated but should be flagged as **traditional**.
- **E. S. Loomis, 1907/1927** — not a proof author but the great compiler;
  *The Pythagorean Proposition* gathers 367 proofs and the four-class taxonomy.
  Loomis's **claim that no trigonometric proof can exist is a documented error**,
  later refuted.
- **Jason Zimba, 2009** — *Forum Geometricorum*, "On the Possibility of
  Trigonometric Proofs of the Pythagorean Theorem," refuting Loomis's claim
  (Family E1). Properly attributed and dated.
- **Calcea Johnson & Ne'Kiya Jackson, 2023 (AMS Spring Southeastern Sectional
  talk) / 2024 (*The American Mathematical Monthly*)** — new trigonometric
  proof(s) via the Law of Sines (Family E2). High-school students from St. Mary's
  Academy, New Orleans. Properly attributed and recently published.

## Misattribution flags (summary)

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Pythagoras proved it | **Legend** — no contemporary source; later tradition only |
| Babylonians/Egyptians proved it | **False** — they knew the *relation*, no proof |
| "Einstein's proof" is original to Einstein | **Anecdotal** — argument is the classical similarity proof |
| Loomis: "no trigonometric proof is possible" | **Documented error** — refuted by Zimba (2009), Jackson–Johnson (2024) |
| Garfield invented his trapezoid proof (1876) | **Documented and correct** |
| Zhao Shuang's dissection proof | **Documented** (3rd c. CE commentary) |
